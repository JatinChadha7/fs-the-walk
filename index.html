Stop 00 — before you walk

Learn the child's name.
Then use it fourteen times.

Everything else in this booklet is secondary to this page. Before the tour begins, you should already know what this child loves. The inquiry form asks a few questions — but the form is not the point. Have a real conversation with the parent about what their child is actually like.

Then walk the tour weighted toward that child. A gymnast lingers at the gymnastics stadium. A reader lingers in the library. A footballer lingers on the turf. Same route, different emphasis.

And say the name out loud, at every stop where it fits. Not "students enjoy this space" — "Anvika will love this." The parent is not buying a building. They are trying to picture their daughter inside it.

So you refer to the child's name through the inquiry — so that it also creates that personal touch, so that they can see their child there in that space.

The idea underneath the whole campus

Four kinds of space

If you understand one concept from the founder's tour, make it this one. It explains why the campus looks the way it does, and it turns "here is a nice area" into "here is a deliberate decision."

David Thornburg's theory of learning spaces, borrowed from a jungle. Any good learning environment needs all four. You will point at all four today.

Campfire

One wise person, a group of learners. The classroom, the teacher, the story being told.

Watering hole

Where everyone gathers informally. The cafe, the lawn, the courts, the corridors between blocks.

Cave

Solitude. Where a child works alone and thinks alone. Block play is the purest example here.

Life

All of it intermingling — and learning applied in the real world. The trips. The D-Mart run.

Where exactly does Namaskar sit in this sequence?

01

The Atrium

Block 1 · GroundWatering holeHigh school parents

Start here and start with a confession: the cafe was not in the original plan. It was added because the school watched its high schoolers and realised they had free slots and nowhere to be together. So the school built them somewhere.

Same story with the indoor calisthenics area, built two or three years ago. It was designed for younger children. The Class 11 and 12 students took it over — they climb on the bars, and they are the ones who use it most. Say this out loud to parents of teenagers. It lands.

Point at

  • The cafe — a hit with students and staff
  • The chess board and the calisthenics bars
  • The senior school offices and the career counselling department
  • The well-being counsellors' room

Career counselling guides students into top universities in India and abroad. But do not rush past the well-being team — there is a dedicated well-being counsellor, regular well-being checks with students, and counsellors students can reach on their own initiative.

Tum bacchon ko thoda kam padhao ge to bhi chalega — but they should be happy, and their safety should be taken care of.

Co-founder Parag sir, quoted by the founder

That is the sentence to remember for the entire tour. Parents trust the school with their children, and the school treats that as the first responsibility, before academics.

If they ask

"Can children skip class and sit in the cafe?"

No. Break times are monitored — there are staff who move students along once their break is over. Free slots are real; bunking is not.

"Why does a school need a cafe at all?"

This is your first chance to use the watering hole idea. Older children need a place to gather informally, and their need for it grows as they grow. School meals are provided — but teenagers sometimes want to eat with friends, on their own terms.

04

Where learning leaves the building

LifeExperiential learning

At the display of trip photographs, slow right down. Experiential learning is one of the core philosophies of the school, and this is where you make it concrete.

The trips themselves: treks, bridge walks, watching a sunset somewhere far from Surat. But the real argument is not the destination. It is that a child packs their own bag, looks after their own belongings, wakes when the bell rings, and stays somewhere far simpler than where they'd stay on a family holiday. That is what builds independence.

Nature camps usually run in December or January. They are optional — but recommended. Safety is planned for in detail, and parents get full information closer to the date.

The story to tell — D-Mart, Jahangirpura

  • Class 2 students are learning addition.
  • They plan a list — a box of colours, a pencil, a packet of chips, something for their mother.
  • They bring ₹500 from home.
  • They walk into D-Mart, fill the cart, and do the billing themselves.
  • They are seven years old, and they come back feeling responsible.
We don't have to think about learning or schooling as something that just happens in the classroom. It's happening all around us — and how can the school enable that?

Class 2 students at the D-Mart billing counter. If this photo exists, it is the single most persuasive image in the entire tour.

06

Early years — Hakuna Matata, and how language works

Blocks 4–6 · GroundJunior & senior KGMontessori-inspired

The early years play area is called Hakuna Matata. There is another called Funky Monkey, and the gymnastics stadium is here too. In early years the focus is play, outdoors, nearly every day — in the rain they put on raincoats and go out anyway.

Inside, the junior and senior KG programme is built on literacy, numeracy and play, inspired by Maria Montessori's approach. Frame it this way: a lot of academic rigour, and no stress. Both, at once.

Then give parents the single most useful idea in the early years wing — language is the vehicle. A child who is weak in language struggles in every other subject. A child who is strong in language gets better at all of them. That is why so much attention goes here.

If any child is language poor, they will struggle in all other subjects. But if your language is strong, then they get better in all the other subjects.

The founder

Many Fountainhead families are first-generation English speakers. The school makes English the primary spoken and academic language — while honouring every home and family language a child brings.

The language map, in one breath

  • Gujarati — offered till Class 8
  • Hindi — all the way to Class 12
  • French — introduced in the senior grades, taught by a native French speaker
  • Sequence taught deliberately: listen, then speak, then read, then write

If they ask

"Why don't you offer Spanish or Chinese?"

Two honest answers, in this order. First, the IB does not require a foreign language at all. Second — and this is the one that earns trust — finding genuinely good native-speaker teachers of Spanish or Chinese in Surat is not realistic, and the school would rather offer nothing than offer it badly. What India does require is a regional language and a second language: hence Gujarati and Hindi. And for children growing up in Surat, those two plus strong English is the genuinely useful combination.

"How do you know that language sequence works?"

Use the founder's own example: she came to Surat without Gujarati. Today she speaks it and understands it functionally, but still cannot read or write it — because she learned it by listening and speaking first. That is how every one of us learned our first language, and it is what the curriculum mirrors.

07

The block play room

Block 6 · GroundCaveRare in India

This is the stop most guides undersell. Do not. The founder singled it out as something she does not believe many schools in India offer at all.

It is a sensorial room inspired by Maria Montessori's work, used by the youngest children. Entirely student-directed: a child arrives, chooses the material they want that day, and sits with it. The teacher demonstrates how the material works, then steps back.

Here is the argument to make. A school day is relentlessly social — group work, classmates, noise, other people. Block play is the one part of the day that is solitary. It is "me" time, and for a child who is an introvert, it is the thing that lets them settle.

They play with one toy for twenty, thirty, forty minutes. And we all know that children's attention spans are going down very rapidly.

The founder

Say the number. Twenty, thirty, forty minutes with a single material. Every parent in the room is worried about their child's attention span, and this is the most direct answer the school has to that worry.

Point at

  • The materials laid out at child height, for the child to choose from
  • One child working alone — if a class is in session
  • The absence of a teacher directing the room

08

The grey building

ArchitectureIGBC Platinum

Some parents find the exposed concrete cold on first sight. Do not apologise for it. Explain it — this is one of the moments where a tour guide either sounds like a brochure or sounds like they understand the school.

The campus was designed by an award-winning architect based in Ahmedabad who works largely in concrete. Co-founder Parag is an architect at heart — a diamond businessman by profession — and he believes in concrete as a material. What you are looking at is exposed concrete structure. It is what it is: no facade, no cladding, no cosmetic layer.

Yes, there are cracks. Yes, some parts look run down. That is the point.

We don't have to necessarily have a facade. We can be what we are, we can be authentic — and we can be beautiful in that way also. I can guarantee you: the grey building grows on you.

The founder

Then land it: the building is deliberately colourless, because the children are the colour. Their work, their displays, their uniforms, their noise. The grey is the canvas.

And there is a hard credential to close with — the campus is an IGBC-certified green building, at Platinum level. No cosmetic paint, no unnecessary chemicals, and a genuine environmental standard behind it.

If they ask

"Why does the building look unfinished?"

It isn't unfinished — it's unclad. Exposed concrete is the finished material, chosen deliberately by the architect and the co-founder. And the honest version: the school would rather spend on a ₹3 crore turf and a library programme than on a facade.

"The building looks old in places."

Parts of it are around eighteen years old and it is ageing. There is a plan to refurbish sections of it. Say that directly — it is far stronger than pretending not to notice.

The turf, the track, the nets

SportsStudent voice

This is the most quietly impressive stretch of the campus, and the one you are most likely to walk too fast.

The turf is all-weather, built to FIFA standards, with material brought from the Netherlands. There was a ground here before, but it could not be used through the rainy season — so it was replaced. It cost around three crore rupees.

Judgement call

Do you tell parents the ₹3 crore figure?

The founder shared it with the team and said openly that she wasn't sure whether it should go to parents. Treat it as your call, read the family, and lean towards not leading with it. The stronger version of the same point is the ISSO story below — it proves the quality without naming a price.

The International Schools Sports Organisation — the network that runs tournaments between IB schools — repeatedly asks Fountainhead to host football, because of this turf. Thirty to forty teams come from IB schools across India. Pathways. Others of that standing. Parents travel from Bombay to watch their children play here.

The kids from other IB schools, when they come here and they see this turf, they tell us: "Wow, you have this." So sometimes we take it for granted. But no — we don't take it for granted.

The founder

Point at, in this order

  • The all-weather FIFA-standard turf
  • The 100-metre synthetic athletics track
  • The cricket nets — and tell the story below
  • The badminton court — and tell that story too
  • The pickleball courts, popular with high schoolers

The story that matters more than the facilities. Fountainhead did not offer cricket for a long time. Students asked, repeatedly, and the school started it three years ago — it is now hugely popular. Badminton was not popular either, until Saina Nehwal and P. V. Sindhu changed that; students asked, and the court was built. Pickleball, the same. Three sports on this campus exist because children asked for them.

That is not a sports fact. That is proof that students have a voice in this school, and that the adults act on it.

Results worth knowing

  • State-level football winners; students have represented Gujarat nationally

  • Students have played internationally, and been shortlisted for India in basketball
  • Around ten sports offered in total

If they ask

"My child trains seriously outside school. Will they be marked absent?"

No. The school has a separate attendance category for students attending training or sports camps — precisely so that a child pursuing their own development is not recorded as simply having taken leave. This answer wins sporty families.

"Is there a swimming pool?"

Not on this campus — the existing infrastructure genuinely does not allow it, and the school is candid that it would like one. There is a pool at the Malgama campus. Do not improvise around this; the straight answer is the right one. One caution: there is a Splash Pool in Block 4 for the early years. If a parent has read that on the signage, clear up the difference before they leave, or they will arrive at admission expecting swimming.

10

The people behind the doors

Day Care · Block 6 · FirstFacultyStaff crèche

Two things here, and both are about staff rather than students — which is exactly why they persuade parents.

First, the staff crèche. The school runs a facility for employees' own young children, with a dedicated team of teachers and health workers. It is one of the biggest reasons people choose to work at Fountainhead, and the reason the school has an unusually strong record of women returning after maternity leave. Very few schools in India offer this to their own staff. A child who starts in the crèche often goes on to enrol in the school itself.

Why does a parent care? Because staff retention is what gives their child continuity.

Second, the depth of tenure. The head of school joined in 2009 — as a Class 1 teacher. Others have been here since 2008. There are long-serving people across administration and support staff. That continuity is what builds a culture rather than just a payroll.

We don't find the best teachers. We make IB teachers.

The founder

This is the line for the most common sceptical question a parent has. The school runs an intensive internal teacher training programme with a dedicated track for IB teaching. The practical benefit: if a teacher becomes unavailable mid-year, there are trained people who can step in — the child's year does not fall apart.

Credentials to have ready

  • Staff members belong to the IB Educator Network
  • The school hosts IB coordinators' meetings across India and South Asia
  • At the IB Global Conference in Mumbai, March 2026: 41 presenters across all IB schools — nine of them were from Fountainhead. Every other school sent one or two.

If they ask

"What is your teacher attrition?"

Around fifteen to twenty percent a year, which is the standard range for this industry. Give the number. A guide who answers this honestly is more credible for the rest of the tour than one who deflects.

MLC — the Maverick Learning Centre

Block 1 · FirstClass 5–12Self-directed

The best single sentence for this space: an unschool inside a regular school. It was set up in-house in 2019 and has run ever since — this is not a pilot.

MLC exists for students who are highly motivated and know what they want to work on. It runs as a mixed-age programme from Class 5 to Class 12, with around seventy students. Learning is largely self-directed and project-based — while language and mathematics continue to be taken care of.

What students have actually done here

  • Coding projects and social entrepreneurship
  • One student built a vegan skincare brand
  • Musicians and serious athletes who train heavily outside school

That last group matters. For a child training seriously in a sport or an instrument, MLC is what keeps them connected to and belonging in the school rather than drifting out of it.

Some students stay two or three years; some stay from Class 5 to Class 12. Many move on after Class 10 into the IB Diploma, and are supported through that transition.

The uncomfortable ones

Six questions you will be asked,
and the honest answer to each

None of these have a defensive answer. Each of them has a true one that is more persuasive than a deflection would be.

"Your school hours are longer than others."

Yes, they are, and the school believes they're right. The real question is what the alternative looks like: a child at home, with screen time as the main challenge, and parents shuttling them between outside classes anyway. Here, those hours are spent safely, with friends, without distractions, and with the child's holistic development taken care of in one place.

"There doesn't seem to be much discipline here."

Compared to a conventional school, it will look that way — and the school owns it. The aim is not a culture of fear but a culture of respect and expression. Students find their leaders approachable. A child spends six to eight hours a day here; they need to be free in their head. But be clear: students who don't follow rules do face consequences.

"Why should I believe your teachers are good?"

Because they were built here. The school does not compete for a small pool of ready-made IB teachers — it runs an intensive training programme and makes them, with a dedicated IB track. The evidence is external: nine of the 41 presenters at the March 2026 IB Global Conference in Mumbai came from Fountainhead.

"Is there a swimming pool?"

Not at this campus. The infrastructure does not allow it and the school would like to have one. There is a pool at the other campus. Say it plainly and move on.

"Aren't you limited, being in Surat?"

This is the question the founder cares most about. The school's answer is to work deliberately with national and international experts — the literacy consultant behind the library programme, the international architecture practice behind the diploma building — so that a Fountainhead student isn't getting less than a student in Mumbai, Delhi or Bangalore. The rankings back it up: fifth nationally in 2025, fourth in 2026 — one point off first.

"When will the new campus get its IB affiliation?"

Handle this one carefully — authorisation is programme-specific, and the timeline runs across roughly two to three years, with visits happening after the school is operating. Do not improvise the detail. Malgama opened in 2025 and is intended to be a full IB continuum school. Confirm the current authorisation position with the academic team before you take an inquiry for that campus.

How to finish

Don't end with a summary. End with their child's name.

Walk them back and tell them the one thing you noticed about their child from the conversation you had at the start — and the one space on this campus you think that child would head for first. That is what they will repeat to each other in the car.

Who said north was up?

Ten things about this school that flip what a parent walks in expecting. Learn these, and you will never need a script.

What this is

Ten claims, each one counter to what a parent assumes when they arrive. Read one before a tour, or all ten in about eight minutes.

How to use it

01Teachers

They assume → a good school hires the best teachers

We don't find the best teachers. We make them.

This is the question underneath a lot of parental scepticism, and Fountainhead's answer is unusually direct. The school does not compete for a thin national pool of ready-made IB teachers. It runs an intensive internal training programme with a dedicated track for IB teaching, and builds them.

There is a practical benefit worth naming to parents: because so many staff are trained to teach IB courses, a teacher becoming unavailable mid-year does not derail a child's year. Someone trained can step in.

We don't find the best teachers in school. But we make IB teachers.The founder

02The building

They assume → the grey concrete looks unfinished

The building has no facade. That is the decision, not the budget.

Designed by an award-winning Ahmedabad architect who works largely in concrete, and shaped by a co-founder who is an architect at heart and a diamond businessman by profession. What a parent sees is exposed concrete structure — the finished material, not a surface waiting to be covered.

There are cracks. Some parts look worn. Do not hurry a parent past that. The building is deliberately colourless because the children are the colour — their work, their displays, their noise fill it in.

We can be what we are, we can be authentic, and we can be beautiful in that way also. I can guarantee you: the grey building grows on you.The founder

The grey is also why the naming matters. The library is Adhyayan. The art room is Rangmanch. The design lab is Schedio, the amphitheatre Anfiteatro, the dining hall Itadakimasu, the diploma wings Atlas and Prometheus. Sanskrit, Hindi, Greek, Italian, Japanese and Latin on one campus, and a child walks past all of it daily.

The environment is called the third teacher. So how is the school teaching you?The founder

03The campus

They assume → open areas are leftover space between buildings

A school needs a campfire, a watering hole, a cave and a life.

This is the single idea that explains the whole campus, borrowed from David Thornburg's theory of learning spaces and its jungle metaphor. Learn it once and every open area on the tour becomes a decision rather than a gap.

Campfire — one wise person, a group of learners. The classroom. Watering hole — where everyone gathers informally: the cafe, the lawn, the courts, the corridors. Cave — solitude, where a child works alone. Life — all of it intermingling, and learning applied in the real world.

A child's need for the watering hole grows as they get older. That is why a cafe was added for high schoolers, and why the diploma wing has informal seating built into it.

Any good learning space should have all these elements.The founder

04Experiential learning

They assume → learning happens in classrooms

The best maths lesson of the year happens at D-Mart.

Class 2 students are learning addition. They plan a list — a box of colours, a pencil, a packet of chips, something for their mother. They bring ₹500 from home. They walk into the D-Mart on this road, fill a cart, and do the billing themselves.

They are seven years old. They come back having handled real money in a real shop, and feeling responsible for it.

Tell this story rather than saying the words "experiential learning." It does the same job and a parent will remember it in the car on the way home.

We don't have to think about learning as something that just happens in the classroom. It's happening all around us — and how can the school enable that?The founder

05Block play

They assume → a good school day is busy and social

Sometimes the most valuable thing a child does all day is sit alone.

The sensorial block play room is inspired by Maria Montessori's work and is entirely student-directed. A child arrives, chooses their material for that day, and sits with it. The teacher demonstrates how it works, then steps back.

The argument to make: a school day is relentlessly social — group work, classmates, constant company. Block play is the one solitary part of it. For an introverted child it is the thing that lets them settle. This is the cave.

They play with one toy for twenty, thirty, forty minutes. And we all know that children's attention spans are going down very rapidly.The founder

Say that number out loud. Every parent in the room is worried about attention spans, and this is the school's most direct answer to that worry.

Few schools in India offer this at all 20–40 minutes of sustained single-material focus Completely student-directed
06Student voice

They assume → the school decides what it offers

Cricket, badminton and pickleball exist here because children asked.

Fountainhead didn't offer cricket for years. Students kept asking, and it started three years ago — now it is one of the most popular things on campus. Badminton was not popular either, until Saina Nehwal and P. V. Sindhu changed that; students asked, and a court was built. Pickleball, the same, for the high schoolers.

Three sports on this campus exist because children requested them and adults acted. That is not a sports fact — it is the clearest available evidence that students have a voice here and that it carries weight.

The idea is not to cultivate a culture of fear, but a culture of respect — and a culture of expression, where students feel empowered to speak.The founder

Pair it with the discipline question when it comes. Compared with a conventional school it can look loose, and the school owns that: it wants students who lead and who have a voice. But students who break rules do face consequences.

~10 sports offered Students find their school leaders approachable Inquiry is the IB's primary pedagogy
07Wellbeing

They assume → wellbeing talk means academics are weak

Happy first, safe first. The results come anyway.

The school's own co-founder tells staff that teaching the children a little less is acceptable — what is not negotiable is that they are happy and that their safety is handled. There is a dedicated well-being counsellor, regular well-being checks, and counsellors students can approach themselves.

Then, in the same breath, give the academic answer — because parents need both halves.

08Language

They assume → more languages offered means a better school

Language isn't a subject. It's the vehicle for every other subject.

A child who is weak in language struggles in every subject. A child who is strong in language gets better at all of them. That is why the early years put so much weight here — and it is a more useful thing to tell a parent than a list of language options.

Many Fountainhead families are first-generation English speakers. The school makes English the primary spoken and academic language while genuinely honouring the home languages children bring with them.

And when a parent asks why there is no Spanish or Chinese, give both honest reasons: the IB does not require a foreign language, and finding truly good native-speaker teachers of those languages in Surat is not realistic. The school would rather not offer something it cannot do well.

When I came to Surat I didn't know Gujarati. Today I'm decently functional — but I still can't read or write it. That's how language development happens.The founder
Listen → speak → read → write Gujarati to Class 8 · Hindi to Class 12 French taught by a native speaker
09Standing

They assume → a Surat school can't match Mumbai or Bangalore

Being in Surat is not a ceiling.

This is the anxiety sitting underneath a great many parent questions, whether or not they say it out loud. The founder names it as one of her guiding principles, and the school's response is not reassurance — it is to bring national and international expertise in, deliberately and repeatedly.

Just because we are geographically isolated, I don't want our students to be limited in any way.The founder

The evidence is competitive, not promotional. At the Neev Literature Festival in Bangalore — a K–8 reading competition where students read thirty to forty books to reach the final — Fountainhead has won, and finished second and third in other years, against The Shri Ram School, Oberoi International and schools of that standing.

The library and literacy programme were built with a consultant whose work is used at Dhirubhai Ambani, Oberoi and Adani International. The turf is good enough that the ISSO network repeatedly asks Fountainhead to host football for thirty to forty visiting IB schools.

And the trajectory is the strongest version of this argument. Fifth nationally in the 2025 Cfore rankings. Fourth in 2026, one point behind first. A school in Surat, closing on the best in the country.

10The people

They assume → staff welfare has nothing to do with their child

We look after our staff's children too. That's why your child keeps their teachers.

Fountainhead runs a crèche for employees' own young children, staffed by a dedicated team of teachers and health workers. Very few schools in India offer this to their own people. It is one of the biggest reasons staff choose to work here, and the reason the school has an unusually strong record of women returning after maternity leave.

Connect it for the parent: staff retention is what gives their child continuity. And the tenure here is real — the head of school joined in 2009, as a Class 1 teacher. Others have been here since 2008, across teaching, administration and support staff.

Be straight about the other side of it too. Annual teacher attrition runs at fifteen to twenty percent, which is the industry standard. Giving that number honestly makes everything else you have said more believable.

There are many employees here who have been here a long time, and that really aids the continuity, the culture building, the institution building.The founder

The eleventh thing

None of this works unless you know the child's name.

Before the tour, find out what this child loves. Then walk the same route weighted toward them, and say their name at every stop where it fits. Not "students enjoy this space" — "Anvika will love this." The parent is not buying a building. They are trying to picture their daughter inside it.
